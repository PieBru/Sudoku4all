Honesty first

Everything past chapter 04 lives on the far edge of the solving world. These techniques appear in magazines' hardest grids and in championship arms races. Solvers meet them a handful of times a year — and even champions reach for them rarely. Learn the names so the map is familiar; let the game you already own do the rest.

The Tier 5 reference table

One honest line each, faithful to the research brief. No tutorials here — this is the signpost at the edge of town.

NameIn one line
Exocet / Double Exocet Four cells share candidates in a special mirrored shape; heavy logic pins which cells hold the digits.
SK Loop A chain of strong links circling four boxes that removes candidates along its rows and columns.
MSLS (Multi-Sector Locked Sets) Rows, columns, and boxes balance their candidates so tightly that whole digit sets are forced.
POM (Pattern Overlay Method) Every complete placement pattern of one digit is listed; cells no pattern uses are dead.
Tridagon Three digits cycling across four cells in one shape cannot all survive; something must break.
Oddagons Odd-length rings of strong links prove one of their candidates true, killing what it sees.
Whips and Braids Generalized chains with memory — each link remembers and re-checks everything before it.
Forcing nets Every candidate is assumed at once, and the surviving conclusions are read off the grid.
Templates The full legal footprint of one digit, intersected down until it forces itself.
Kraken fish A fish whose fin is not a cell but a whole chain, proven true or false before the catch.

The three great references, if you want the deep versions: SudokuWiki's strategy families, HoDoKu's technique list, and Sudopedia.

How champions actually solve

Here is the open secret: world-class solving is not a secret fourth tier. It is chapters 01 through 03 — singles, pairs, fish, wings — executed at extreme speed with near-zero errors. Chains appear late and briefly.

The marking system with a name is Snyder notation, after champion Thomas Snyder: within one box, pencil a digit only where it is limited to exactly two cells. Corner marks in a box, never full notes. The marks stay few, so scanning stays fast. On genuinely hard grids even Snyder switches to full notes — the system is a speed tool, not a religion.

The habits behind the speed:

  • Pattern memory — shapes are recognized whole, not searched cell by cell.
  • Cheapest-first discipline — rerun the cheap ladder twice before paying for anything hard.
  • Minimal marks — every mark must earn its ink; clutter slows the eye.
  • Composure — under championship pressure, error recovery beats error-free fantasy.

Records, as reported

Two famous times, both reported, not audited here: Thomas Snyder solved a very easy puzzle in 1:23.93 (a different class than competition grids), and Wang Shiyao solved a championship-grade grid in 54.44 seconds at the 2018 World Sudoku and Puzzle Championship. Treat them as folklore with good sources.

Your path from here

You have the whole ladder. What sharpens it is practice with intent: play the game timed once you are comfortable, use the hint button to step through stuck moments, and train one technique at a time.

The end-of-ladder checkpoint

Three last questions, and then the map is yours:

  1. Name any three techniques from the Tier 5 table.
  2. Explain Snyder notation in one sentence to a friend.
  3. Say what champions rely on most — and why it is not a secret tier.
