What you will learn

A note before we start: this chapter talks to grown-up solvers now, so the wording grows up too. You will meet the pair family — naked pairs, hidden pairs, and their bigger siblings — plus two mirror tricks called pointing and claiming.

One new word helps everywhere. A unit is any row, any column, or any 3×3 box — the three places a digit may appear only once. And the small numbers a square could still take are its candidates; in the game they are your notes.

Naked pair

A naked pair is two squares in one unit whose candidate lists are exactly the same two digits. Those two digits belong to those two squares — one each, in some order. Every other square in the unit can forget both digits.

Triples and quads: the same idea, bigger

Nothing changes with more squares. Three squares of one unit carrying only three digits among them make a naked triple; four squares and four digits make a naked quad. Look at the board above and imagine a third square in row 4 holding exactly 3, 7 and one more digit — that trio would lock all three digits, and every other row 4 square would lose them.

Hidden pair

A hidden pair is the same idea seen from the digits' side. Two digits of one unit can only land in the same two squares. Those two squares are now spoken for, so every other candidate inside them can go. The pair was hiding behind extra candidates.

Hidden triples and quads extend the same way: three digits hiding in three squares, four in four. The sentence to remember: naked pairs start from matching squares, hidden pairs start from trapped digits.

Pointing

Pointing and claiming are mirror tricks; both are called locked candidates. For pointing, pick a box and a digit. If every place that digit could sit inside the box lies on one row, the box points at that row: the digit must be there — so the rest of the row drops it.

The same works for columns: if the digit's homes line up on one column of the box, the rest of that column drops it.

Claiming

Claiming runs the mirror: the row forces the box. Pick a row and a digit. If every candidate in the row lies inside one box, the row claims that box — the rest of the box drops the digit.

The mirror sentence, one line each: pointing — the box forces the row; claiming — the row forces the box.

Try it in the game

Open the game, set difficulty to Medium, and press New game. Your first hunt: find one pair — naked or hidden — before anything else. Then find one pointing or claiming pattern and say which it is.

Checkpoint

Can you do these three things?

  1. Find the naked pair in a fresh Medium board and say which two squares own their digits.
  2. Look at the two fragment boards above: one shows pointing, one shows claiming. Which is which, and why?
  3. Say the mirror sentence without looking: who forces whom, in each direction?
