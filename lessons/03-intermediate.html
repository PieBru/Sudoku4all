What you will learn

This chapter expects chapter 02: you read candidate notes comfortably and know pointing and claiming. Here you follow one digit across the whole board. Two families reward that focus: fish, which trap a digit inside a rectangle of rows and columns, and single-digit patterns, which chain two such traps together.

X-Wing

Pick a digit. In two different rows, suppose its candidates sit in exactly the same two columns. Those four cells form a rectangle — an X-Wing. The digit must occupy two diagonal corners, so those two columns are fully served: every other candidate of that digit in both columns can go.

Swap the words rows and columns and the trick still works: two columns locking the same two rows.

Swordfish

The same idea with three rows instead of two. In three rows, the digit's candidates all sit inside the same three columns — a Swordfish. The three corners feed those columns, so all other candidates of the digit in the three columns go. Each row may use only two of the columns; three is the ceiling.

A Jellyfish is the four-row version. It exists, it is legal, and it is rarer than patience — most solvers meet one or two in a lifetime. X-Wing, Swordfish, Jellyfish: two rows, three rows, four rows; one family.

Skyscraper

Single-digit patterns use two parallel lines. A Skyscraper is two columns where the digit has exactly two candidates each, and one pair sits in the same row — the base. The other two cells are the tips. Any cell seeing both tips can drop the digit.

2-String Kite

A 2-String Kite bends the two lines: one string runs along a row, one down a column, and they meet inside a box that holds exactly two candidates of the digit. Each string has exactly one candidate outside the box. The two far ends see each other — or both see one common cell — and that cell drops the digit.

Turbot fish

A Turbot fish is the general shape behind all of these: any chain of alternating strong and weak links on a single digit, closed by two strong links whose ends overlap. The skyscraper and the 2-string kite are its two most common costumes. Finding one is the skill; naming it is bookkeeping.

Related, and worth a name-check: the Empty Rectangle combines a bi-value line with a box whose candidates line up neatly. It works, but a canonical school deprecates it as redundant — other patterns find the same eliminations. Meet it again in the wild before investing practice.

Finned fish, and the stranger seas

Sometimes a fish is perfect except one extra candidate clinging to a corner — a fin. A finned fish keeps almost the old eliminations: everything that sees the fin cell still goes. A sashimi fish is the finned cousin whose corner cell itself is missing. Take the X-Wing board above and imagine a stray 4 highlighted beside a corner: the crossed 4s seeing that stray cell still go, the others are off.

Two more names, no tutorials: franken fish mix rows, columns and boxes as their lines; mutant fish use only boxes and diagonals of boxes. They exist for completeness — see the references in chapter 05 when a puzzle truly demands one.

Wings I: the XY-Wing

A wing is three cells that gang up on a fourth. The XY-Wing needs a hinge holding exactly two candidates, say 3 and 8, plus two pincers. Each pincer sees the hinge and holds one hinge digit plus a shared extra digit — here 5.

Why it works: the hinge is 3 or 8. If 3, the lower pincer is 5; if 8, the upper pincer is 5. Either way a pincer is 5, and any cell seeing both cannot be 5.

Wings II: the W-Wing

The W-Wing swaps the hinge for a strong link: two cells in one line that are the only homes of a digit there. Between them sit two cells with the same pair of candidates.

Read it twice: the link forces one pair-cell to 7; the pair cells force each other; the victim sees both. Two same-pair cells plus one tight column are the whole recipe.

Uniqueness: one answer, a promise

Every puzzle here has exactly one solution — the game engine checks it before a board ships. That promise is a tool. Some positions would allow two answers; since two are forbidden, anything creating them must be wrong.

The simplest trap is the deadly rectangle: four cells in a perfect rectangle, across exactly two boxes, holding the same pair. Filled one way or the other, both stay legal — two answers. Forbidden.

UR type 1: the escape hatch

Unique Rectangle (UR) type 1: three rectangle cells hold only the pair, one holds an extra candidate. To dodge the deadly rectangle, the extra candidate must be true.

The family grows by moving the escape around. One line each:

  • UR type 2 — the extra cell pairs with a fifth cell; one of the two is the extra digit, so cells seeing both lose it.
  • UR type 3 — the extra candidates join a nearby subset, like a phantom naked pair.
  • UR type 4 — a strong link on one pair digit keeps it alive in the extra cell; the other digit there dies.
  • UR type 5 — three cells carry the extra digit; cells seeing all three lose it.
  • UR type 6 — the rectangle sits on two strong links; only one diagonal pairing survives.
  • Hidden UR — strong links on both digits squeeze the rectangle from outside.
  • Avoidable Rectangle — same trap, but built from digits you placed, not printed clues.

BUG+1: the grave

BUG stands for Bivalue Universal Grave — a board where every unsolved cell holds exactly two candidates and every digit pairs off. That shape always has two answers, so it can never occur in a fair puzzle.

BUG+1 is the grave plus one survivor: a single cell with three candidates. Look again at the UR board above for the face of it: pair cells everywhere, one cell with an extra digit. The rule is one sentence — in the three-candidate cell, keep only the digit that appears three times in its row, column and box combined; the puzzle is trying to trap itself into the grave otherwise. Rare, strange, delightful.

Sue de Coq: the rare bird

Sue de Coq — named after its finder — is a locked handshake between a row and a box. Two intersection cells hold four digits between them; a row cell and a box cell each hold two of those digits. Together the four cells consume exactly four digits.

Honestly rare: most solvers meet a handful per year. Knowing the name is enough; the references in chapter 05 show more.

Remote pairs: a chain of twins

A remote pair is a string of cells all holding the same two digits, each cell seeing the next. Neighbours must differ; cells an even number of steps apart must match.

Counting steps is the whole skill. Odd distance apart means opposite digits; any cell seeing both ends of an odd stretch loses the pair.

Try it in the game

Open the game, set difficulty to Expert, and press New game. Hunt a wing before anything harder: scan for two-digit cells, then check whether one of them hinges two others. One found wing saves minutes of staring.

Checkpoint

You graduate from chapter 03 when you can do all of these:

  1. On the X-Wing board, point at the four rectangle cells and say which columns get cleaned.
  2. On the UR type 1 board, say which cell must take its extra candidate — and why the deadly rectangle forbids the pair.
  3. Name the three cells of the XY-Wing board and why the crossed 5 must go.
