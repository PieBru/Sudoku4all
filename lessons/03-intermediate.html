What you will learn

This chapter expects chapter 02: you read candidate notes comfortably and know pointing and claiming. Here you follow one digit across the whole board. Two families reward that focus: fish, which trap a digit inside a rectangle of rows and columns, and single-digit patterns, which chain two such traps together.

X-Wing

Pick a digit. In two different rows, suppose its candidates sit in exactly the same two columns. Those four cells form a rectangle — an X-Wing. The digit must occupy two diagonal corners, so those two columns are fully served: every other candidate of that digit in both columns can go.

Swap the words rows and columns and the trick still works: two columns locking the same two rows.

Swordfish

The same idea with three rows instead of two. In three rows, the digit's candidates all sit inside the same three columns — a Swordfish. The three corners feed those columns, so all other candidates of the digit in the three columns go. Each row may use only two of the columns; three is the ceiling.

A Jellyfish is the four-row version. It exists, it is legal, and it is rarer than patience — most solvers meet one or two in a lifetime. X-Wing, Swordfish, Jellyfish: two rows, three rows, four rows; one family.

Skyscraper

Single-digit patterns use two parallel lines. A Skyscraper is two columns where the digit has exactly two candidates each, and one pair sits in the same row — the base. The other two cells are the tips. Any cell seeing both tips can drop the digit.

2-String Kite

A 2-String Kite bends the two lines: one string runs along a row, one down a column, and they meet inside a box that holds exactly two candidates of the digit. Each string has exactly one candidate outside the box. The two far ends see each other — or both see one common cell — and that cell drops the digit.

Turbot fish

A Turbot fish is the general shape behind all of these: any chain of alternating strong and weak links on a single digit, closed by two strong links whose ends overlap. The skyscraper and the 2-string kite are its two most common costumes. Finding one is the skill; naming it is bookkeeping.

Related, and worth a name-check: the Empty Rectangle combines a bi-value line with a box whose candidates line up neatly. It works, but a canonical school deprecates it as redundant — other patterns find the same eliminations. Meet it again in the wild before investing practice.

Finned fish, and the stranger seas

Sometimes a fish is perfect except one extra candidate clinging to a corner — a fin. A finned fish keeps almost the old eliminations: everything that sees the fin cell still goes. A sashimi fish is the finned cousin whose corner cell itself is missing. Take the X-Wing board above and imagine a stray 4 highlighted beside a corner: the crossed 4s seeing that stray cell still go, the others are off.

Two more names, no tutorials: franken fish mix rows, columns and boxes as their lines; mutant fish use only boxes and diagonals of boxes. They exist for completeness — see the references in chapter 05 when a puzzle truly demands one.

Try it in the game

Open the game, set difficulty to Hard, and press New game. Hunt with one digit at a time: pick a digit, note where it can go in each row and column, and look for a rectangle. Even one found X-Wing pays for the whole search.

Checkpoint (part 1)

Can you do these three things?

  1. On the X-Wing board above, point at the four rectangle cells and say which columns get cleaned.
  2. On the skyscraper board, name the base, the two tips, and why the crossed cells must go.
  3. Say what changes for the eliminations when a fish grows a fin.
