Two kinds of links

A link joins two candidates that argue about one digit in one unit. There are exactly two kinds, and every technique in this chapter is built from them.

Strong says "at least one is true"; weak says "at most one is true". Chains alternate them, and the alternation is where the power comes from.

The AIC: one chain to rule them

An Alternating Inference Chain (AIC) is any path of strong and weak links, alternating, starting and ending strong. Assume the first candidate is false and follow the implications.

Read it as a story: if r1c1 is not 7, it is 1; then r2c3 is not 1, so it is 2; then r2c8 is not 2, so it is 3; then r5c8 is not 3, so it is 7. Either r1c1 is 7 or r5c8 is 7. So r1c8, which sees both, loses the 7.

That is the whole machinery. Coloring, cycles, XY-chains — each is just an AIC wearing a costume.

Simple coloring

Take one digit and chase only its strong links. Two linked cells get opposite colors. Color cells that see each other through a strong link alternately, and the chain colors itself.

The payoff: any cell that sees a yellow 5-chain cell and a blue one — like r1c8, via row 1 and column 8 — sees both families. Whichever family is true, that cell cannot be 5. Crossed it goes.

X-Cycles: the chain bites its tail

An X-Cycle is the same single-digit chase, closed into a loop. Look at the coloring board again and imagine the chain bending until its ends meet: alternating strong and weak links all the way around.

You already know the moves; X-Cycles just names the closed case. Treat it as coloring's formal name on expert forums.

3D Medusa: coloring two dimensions

3D Medusa colors more than one digit at once: a cell's two candidates count as a strong link inside the cell, so the coloring jumps between digits and cells.

Same rule as coloring, bigger web: any candidate seeing both colors of its own digit goes. The tools you own already scale.

XY-Chain: hand-offs between cells

An XY-Chain is an AIC whose strong links all live inside two-candidate cells, and whose weak links live between them. Each cell catches the story and passes it on.

Check each hand-off: the two cells share the digit being dropped, and share a row, column or box. Miss either check and the chain is broken — the elimination worthless.

Grouped chains: cells in formation

A chain node can be a group — several cells in one box that all sit in one line. The group acts as a single candidate: either one of its cells holds the digit, or none does.

The legality rule is the pointing rule from chapter 02: the group must be every 6-candidate of its box inside that line. You have been reading grouped chains since chapter 02 without the name.

ALS: almost locked sets

A locked set is n cells holding exactly n digits — your naked pairs from chapter 02. An almost locked set (ALS) is the loose version: n cells holding exactly n+1 digits. One digit too many — almost tight, almost forced.

The power move is ALS-XZ: two sets sharing two digits. One shared digit, x, is restricted — its cells on both sides all see each other, so only one set can use it. Then the other shared digit, z, must land in one set or the other.

Grow the idea: three sets chained through restricted commons give an ALS chain; two sets plus a hinge give the ALS-XY-Wing. They are the wing and chain ideas you already own, with sets standing in for single cells.

Forcing chains: assume and follow

A forcing chain stops being clever: pick a cell, assume each of its candidates in turn, and follow what follows. If every branch reaches the same conclusion, that conclusion is proven.

The flavors differ only in what branches: a digit forcing chain branches on a digit's two homes, a cell forcing chain on one cell's candidates, a unit forcing chain on every placement in a unit. Nishio is the pessimist's version: assume a candidate is true and hunt for a contradiction. Bowman's Bingo is the same search organized as a tidy tree of consequences — the paperwork version of what you just did.

Death Blossom rare

A Death Blossom has a stem cell and petals. Each petal is an ALS that the stem sees, covering one stem candidate. The stem must be one of its candidates, so one petal always gets tightened.

Aligned Pair Exclusion rare

Aligned Pair Exclusion (APE) takes two cells that see each other and lists their possible value pairs. Any pair that would kill a third cell is impossible. Kill every pair containing some value, and that value is gone.

Rare finds, both of these — a solver meets them a few times a year, on the cruelest boards. Know the names, sleep well.

The escalation order, and the last resort

Cheap first, always: singles, then pairs and intersections, then fish and wings, then chains and sets. Hard techniques take minutes; easy ones take seconds. Scan the cheap ladder twice before paying for anything expensive.

And the honest last resort: if every logical avenue is exhausted, trial — guess a candidate and follow it — reaches the same place an enormous forcing net would. Purists object; pragmatists finish puzzles. The debate is real; the solved grid doesn't care.

Checkpoint

You graduate from chapter 04 when you can do all of these:

  1. Follow the worked AIC chain end to end, saying at each step whether the link is strong or weak and why.
  2. On the ALS-XZ board, name the two sets, the restricted common digit, and what r3c3 loses.
  3. Flip back to chapter 03's XY-Wing board and find the hinge and the two pincers in under a minute.
