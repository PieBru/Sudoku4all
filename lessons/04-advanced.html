Two kinds of links

A link joins two candidates that argue about one digit in one unit. There are exactly two kinds, and every technique in this chapter is built from them.

Strong says "at least one is true"; weak says "at most one is true". Chains alternate them, and the alternation is where the power comes from.

The AIC: one chain to rule them

An Alternating Inference Chain (AIC) is any path of strong and weak links, alternating, starting and ending strong. Assume the first candidate is false and follow the implications.

Read it as a story: if r1c1 is not 7, it is 1; then r2c3 is not 1, so it is 2; then r2c8 is not 2, so it is 3; then r5c8 is not 3, so it is 7. Either r1c1 is 7 or r5c8 is 7. So r1c8, which sees both, loses the 7.

That is the whole machinery. Coloring, cycles, XY-chains — each is just an AIC wearing a costume.

Simple coloring

Take one digit and chase only its strong links. Two linked cells get opposite colors. Color cells that see each other through a strong link alternately, and the chain colors itself.

The payoff: any cell that sees a yellow 5-chain cell and a blue one — like r1c8, via row 1 and column 8 — sees both families. Whichever family is true, that cell cannot be 5. Crossed it goes.

X-Cycles: the chain bites its tail

An X-Cycle is the same single-digit chase, closed into a loop. Look at the coloring board again and imagine the chain bending until its ends meet: alternating strong and weak links all the way around.

You already know the moves; X-Cycles just names the closed case. Treat it as coloring's formal name on expert forums.

3D Medusa: coloring two dimensions

3D Medusa colors more than one digit at once: a cell's two candidates count as a strong link inside the cell, so the coloring jumps between digits and cells.

Same rule as coloring, bigger web: any candidate seeing both colors of its own digit goes. The tools you own already scale.

XY-Chain: hand-offs between cells

An XY-Chain is an AIC whose strong links all live inside two-candidate cells, and whose weak links live between them. Each cell catches the story and passes it on.

Check each hand-off: the two cells share the digit being dropped, and share a row, column or box. Miss either check and the chain is broken — the elimination worthless.

Grouped chains: cells in formation

A chain node can be a group — several cells in one box that all sit in one line. The group acts as a single candidate: either one of its cells holds the digit, or none does.

The legality rule is the pointing rule from chapter 02: the group must be every 6-candidate of its box inside that line. You have been reading grouped chains since chapter 02 without the name.

Try it in the game

Open the game, set difficulty to Expert, and press New game. Before reaching for anything else, pick one digit and color it: chase its strong links, two colors, then hunt for a cell seeing both. One coloring often cracks an expert board's first wall.
